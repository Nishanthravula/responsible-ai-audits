# AI, Labor Transformation and Displacement

**Comparing occupational AI-exposure tools, auditing the Duolingo contractor case, and drafting a worker-centric AI policy**

PhD 832: Ethics in Responsible AI · University of the Cumberlands · September 2026

[Full report (PDF, 20 pages)](./Lab5_Labor_Transformation_Ravula.pdf)

---

## The question

AI-exposure calculators claim to tell workers how much of their job AI can do. Do they agree, and does "exposure" actually predict who gets displaced? When displacement does happen, which legal protections apply and which don't?

## Method

1. **Compare exposure tools.** I ran three occupations (Software Engineer, Business Analyst, Medical Researcher) through two tools: TripleTen's AI exposure calculator and the AI Labor Index. I added a controlled baseline that ran every occupation on the same profile, plus a task-level analysis with a selection-bias check.
2. **Audit a real case.** Using the OECD AI Incidents Monitor and contemporaneous reporting, I reconstructed the **Duolingo contractor reduction (January 2024)**. I separated verified facts, company statements, contractor claims and my own interpretation.
3. **Legal synthesis.** I grounded the analysis in Ponce Del Castillo and Taes (2025), *Artificial Intelligence and Labor Law*, and in ILO Recommendations 166 and 198.
4. **Policy.** I drafted a 500-word worker-centric AI policy and argued a policy position.

## Key findings

**1. The tools agree on the ranking but not on the gaps.** Both put Software Engineer ahead of Business Analyst, with Medical Researcher last. But the AI Labor Index measures what AI *could* do to a job's tasks, while TripleTen also factors in how much AI people actually use. A controlled re-run on identical profiles widened the spread from 26 to 34 points, so my original inputs *understated* the differences between occupations rather than exaggerating them.

**2. Exposure alone doesn't decide the outcome.** Software Engineer and Business Analyst have almost the same automation score (3.10 vs. 3.09), but observed AI adoption differs about 22-fold. None of the three roles looks likely to disappear. All three look set to be restructured.

**3. Duolingo shows cognitive displacement.** About 10% of contractors were offboarded at the end of 2023 (reported January 2024), and the company attributed part of the reduced need to AI. No full-time employees were affected. The work affected was centered on translation: skilled, cognitive work, not routine tasks.

**4. Protections were weakest exactly where the displacement happened.** The affected workers were contractors, often outside consultation and redundancy protections. The EU AI Act's duty to inform workers (Art. 26(7)) covers only high-risk systems such as hiring tools, not content generation. And Duolingo is a US company. EU law is therefore a benchmark for good labor governance here, not a rule that bound Duolingo.

**5. Policy position: it's a governance problem when the gain is taken out of workers without their say.** AI-enabled automation stops being just a productivity improvement when an organization turns the time AI saves into less work, less pay or a lower-skilled role, and decides that on its own. The worker-centric policy turns this into concrete triggers:
- A **material-change trigger**: AI expected to take over 20% or more of a role's working time, measured by a task-frequency audit and validated by workers
- **60 days' written notice**, and an exit notice that says so whenever AI contributed to ending a contract
- A joint **displacement and deskilling assessment**, plus consultation with a worker panel that includes contractor representatives (ILO R166)
- An **ILO R198 misclassification check** before ending any contract that has run for 12 months or more
- Mandatory expert human review of AI output, and 12 months of post-deployment monitoring
